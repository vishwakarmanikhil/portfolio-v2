The right web developer for your business is the one who **asks about your business before talking about technology**, can **show you live work** similar to what you need, puts **the scope and price in writing**, and makes sure **you own your website, domain and data**. Price matters, but a cheap website that brings no enquiries, or that you can't change later, is the most expensive option.

Here are the questions I'd ask if I were hiring a developer, and what a good answer sounds like.

## 1. Can I see live websites or apps you have built?

Ask for links you can open and use, not only screenshots. Click around on your phone. Is it fast? Is it easy to use? Do the forms work?

**Good sign:** live links to work similar to yours, ideally for businesses like yours. You can see mine on my [freelance page](/freelance#client-work).

## 2. What do you need to know about my business before quoting?

A developer who quotes before understanding your customers, your goals and your process is guessing.

**Good sign:** they ask who your customers are, how you get business today, what the website must achieve, and what you use now (Excel, WhatsApp, an old site).

## 3. Will I get a written scope?

The scope is the list of pages, features and integrations that are included, and what is **not** included.

**Good sign:** a written document with the scope, timeline, price and payment milestones, before any work starts. I explain what should be in it in [what decides the cost of a website or app](/blog/what-decides-the-cost-of-a-website-or-app).

## 4. Who will own the domain, hosting, code and data?

This is the question people regret not asking. If the developer registers your domain in their own name, or keeps the hosting and code, you can be stuck with them.

**Good sign:** the domain and hosting accounts are in **your** name, and you receive the code and admin access at the end.

## 5. How will the website bring in enquiries?

A website is a sales tool, not a brochure.

**Good sign:** they talk about clear calls to action, short forms, WhatsApp, speed on mobile, trust and proof, and what happens to an enquiry after it arrives. I cover this in [why a website doesn't bring in enquiries](/blog/why-your-website-is-not-bringing-enquiries).

## 6. Will it work well on phones and load quickly?

Most of your visitors will be on a phone.

**Good sign:** they build and test for mobile first, and can tell you how they keep the site fast (optimised images, not loading unnecessary scripts).

## 7. Will Google and AI assistants be able to read it?

**Good sign:** they mention page titles and descriptions, a sitemap, structured data, Google Search Console, and making sure the text is in the page itself. If you serve local customers, they should mention your Google Business Profile too. See [how to show up on Google for local customers](/blog/show-up-on-google-local-customers).

## 8. Can I update content myself?

**Good sign:** they ask what you'll want to change (prices, projects, photos, blog posts) and set up an easy way to do it, or agree on how changes will be handled.

## 9. How will we communicate during the project?

**Good sign:** a clear contact person, regular updates, and a **preview link** where you can see the site as it is being built and give feedback early, not only at the end.

## 10. What happens after launch?

Websites need occasional fixes, updates and changes.

**Good sign:** a clear answer on support: what is included, for how long, and how further changes are priced.

## What are the red flags?

Be careful if a developer:

- Gives you a price **before asking a single question**.
- Has **no live work** you can open.
- Promises **"guaranteed first page on Google"**. Nobody can honestly guarantee rankings.
- Won't put the **scope in writing**.
- Keeps the **domain, hosting or code** in their own name.
- **Disappears** for days during the sales process. It rarely gets better once you've paid.

## Is a freelancer or an agency better?

Both can be good. A **freelancer** usually means you work directly with the person building your site, often at a lower cost and with faster decisions. An **agency** can bring a larger team for very big projects. What matters more is the answers to the ten questions above.
