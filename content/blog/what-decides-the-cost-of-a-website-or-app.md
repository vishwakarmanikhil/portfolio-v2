"How much will it cost?" is the first question almost every client asks, and the honest answer is: **it depends on how many different things the software has to do**. Not the number of pages, and not how modern it looks. A 20-page website with only information can cost less than a 3-screen app that takes payments.

This post explains what actually changes the price, what to prepare before you ask for a quote, and how to spot a quote you shouldn't trust.

## What makes a website or app cost more?

These are the factors that matter most, roughly in order:

1. **Logins and user roles.** As soon as people log in, someone has to build accounts, passwords, permissions and security. Different roles (admin, employee, client) each see different things.
2. **Custom logic.** Booking rules, pricing calculations, approval flows and allocation rules are where most development time goes.
3. **Integrations.** Payments (Razorpay, PhonePe), WhatsApp messages, email, maps and other software each add work and testing.
4. **Admin panels and reports.** Managing data, filtering it and exporting it is often half of a business system.
5. **Design.** A custom design takes longer than adapting a clean, proven layout.
6. **Content.** Writing text and preparing photos is real work. If it isn't ready, the project waits.
7. **Platforms.** A website, a web app, an Android app and an iOS app are four different targets. React Native can cover both phones from one codebase.
8. **Deadline.** A rushed timeline costs more and usually means cutting something.

## What usually doesn't change the cost much?

- **The number of simple information pages**, once the design is done.
- **Using a modern framework.** Tools like Next.js often make projects faster, not more expensive.
- **Being mobile-friendly.** That should be standard in every quote, not an extra.

## What should you prepare before asking for a quote?

You will get a faster, more accurate quote if you can answer:

- **What problem are you solving?** "Leads get lost" is more useful than "I need a CRM".
- **Who will use it?** Visitors, customers, your employees, or all three.
- **What must it do on day one,** and what can wait for later?
- **What do you use today?** Excel, WhatsApp, an old website. This shows the real process.
- **Examples you like**, and what you like about them.
- **Your rough budget and deadline.** Sharing a range lets a developer suggest the best option inside it, instead of guessing.

## What should a good quote include?

A quote you can trust is a **written scope**, not just a number. It should list:

- The pages, screens and features included.
- What is **not** included.
- The timeline and the milestones where you will review progress.
- Who provides content, domain and hosting, and who pays for third-party services.
- What happens after launch: fixes, support and changes.

## What are the warning signs in a quote?

Be careful if:

- **The price comes before any questions.** Nobody can price software they don't understand.
- **There is no written scope.** Disagreements later are almost guaranteed.
- **"Unlimited changes"** is promised. It usually means the scope was never defined.
- **You won't own the code, domain or hosting accounts.** Make sure they are in your name.
- **There is nothing live to look at.** Ask for past work you can actually open and use.

## How can you reduce the cost?

- **Start with the smallest version that solves the problem**, then add features once people are using it.

- **Have your content ready** before development starts.
- **Make decisions quickly.** Waiting time stretches every project.
